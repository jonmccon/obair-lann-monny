Solo designer for a 200-person, $7M MRR national tax relief company. I owned the brand, every marketing surface, and the internal product simultaneously, for about a year, and built a design system that tied all three together.

- **Role:** Brand & Product Designer
- **Company:** Priority Tax Relief, national tax attorney services firm 
- **Timeline:** 2025–2026
- **Platform:** Responsive web, digital advertising, email, print, broadcast TV, social media, internal CRM
- **Team:** Solo designer embedded with engineering, marketing, and senior leadership
- **Tools:** Figma, Adobe Suite, Claude, VS Code, Jupyter Notebooks, Django/Wagtail, WordPress, npm, Microsoft Office
- **Outcome:** Shipped rebrand, design system released, replatformed website, Chrome extension, internal CRM prototype.

## Two briefs wearing the same coat

The brand was fragmented and inconsistent across every channel I could find: TV spots, PPC ads, website, emails, letterhead, merch. It had good ideas and decent bones, but no steward, no guidelines, no system. That inconsistency was bleeding credibility in a category where trust is the entire product. There was a real opportunity to raise the professional bar above competitors and build something the internal teams could rally around.

Internally, the employees were drowning in a stack of tools they'd outgrown. Getting one case from intake to resolution required switching between half a dozen contexts and manually moving data that should have just been *there*. The ideas behind the workflows were sound. The execution had hit a ceiling.

Fix the face. Fix the engine. Both at once. Solo.

## My Role

Priority brought me in as their first dedicated designer with a specific mandate: build the design infrastructure the company had been operating without. I worked directly with the ownership group, embedded with engineering, marketing, IT, and senior leadership simultaneously.

My contribution was the throughline across all three tracks in brand, product, and marketing, owned from brief to delivery. I set the visual direction, ran the research, made the system decisions, and produced the final artifacts. Every surface the company touched externally or internally went through me.

## Starting with Research, Not Opinions

Before touching anything I needed to understand the landscape. I ran a full multi-platform brand audit on what existed and what competitors were doing. Then I did something I don't think gets done enough at this category level: I analyzed the actual customer data. Pulled the full paying customer database into a Jupyter notebook and mapped it properly. Demographics, debt range, geography, credit profiles. Real numbers, not guesses.

## The Brand System

The logo wasn't a from-scratch redesign, it was a refinement and systematization of an existing concept. The hawk mark had presence. What it needed were tighter geometry, consistent construction rules, and a full system of lockups that would work from a broadcast lower-third to a Chrome extension favicon.

I explored a wide range before narrowing down, including a serif collegiate direction that got pretty far along before being retired, an eagle variant, several weight and spacing variations. The key decision was moving from the existing busy treatment to a cleaner, more confident mark.

<div class="two-column">

{% image "./PTR-130-moodboard.png", "Brand images moodboard." %}

{% image "./PTR-134-brand-merch-ideas.png", "Brand extension, color variations of the cap mockup for seasonal campaigns." %}

</div>

The Marlboro Cup racing program from 1987 isn't obvious reference material for a tax firm. But it captured exactly the feeling we were after: authority without stuffiness, the kind of visual confidence that makes a nervous customer feel like they're in capable hands. The moodboard grounded the brand direction in that specific register, confident, direct, American, and gave the team a shared reference point for every creative decision that followed.

From there I built out the full brand platform: color system, typography, voice and copywriting direction, imagery guidelines, supporting brand elements, and complete usage guidelines.

The orange-and-green palette that existed before had the right instincts around warmth and trust, but needed tighter contrast ratios, AA accessibility compliance, and clear production specs for every output: CMYK for print, RGB/HEX for screen, Pantone for broadcast.

Everything got collected into a brand guidelines document and a formal brand book, handed to stakeholders as the source of truth.

## Operations: the Surface Designers Usually Skip

The brand guidelines covered external-facing work, but I also extended the system into the operational layer: the documents employees use every day that shape how the company presents itself in every client interaction.

The most impactful was letterhead. PTR had 80+ Word documents that all had their own ad-hoc header: screenshots of the logo at different resolutions, inconsistent address formatting, no grid. I redesigned the system as proper Word templates: embedded fonts, dynamic fields for case number and date, structured layout, consistent footer with address and pagination. Then rebuilt all 80+ documents using the new template.

## The First Product Move: a Chrome Extension

Rather than blowing up the existing Logics CRM and replacing it, we started with a targeted intervention. I designed a Chrome extension that injected new functionality directly into the Logics CRM interface no migration, no retraining, just new capability sitting on top of what people already knew how to use.

The idea was to start *putting the data to work for people* rather than making people shepherd it from one system to another. That framing, data working for you, became the product philosophy going forward.

{% image "./PTR-10-chrome-plugin-design-Logics-Extension-Menu-77-11796.png", "Chrome extension design, the Logics CRM with the new PTR extension menu injected into the interface. Additional actions and data visibility added at the point of use. The 'minimum viable improvement' move before a full platform rebuild." %}

This was an appropriately humble first step. It proved the concept, got buy-in from users who saw immediate value, and gave us a real behavioral baseline before designing the next phase.

## Reflection

There was a lot of ground to cover and I moved through it fast. But I don't think I brought the ownership group along with me consistently enough.

The gap wasn't the work. It was the governance model for how decisions got surfaced. I was operating at a design-strategy pace, making dozens of decisions a day to maintain momentum. The ownership group wanted to be present for ground-level decisions. Neither was wrong. But without an explicit agreement upfront about what I'd own autonomously and what needed their input, that difference in operating tempo became friction.

What I'd do differently: establish a decision-rights framework at the start of any embedded solo engagement. What I decide, what I present, how often, and what requires sign-off before moving. That structure protects both sides.

The system thinking was right. The stakeholder communication needed more structure.
